How It Works

1. Preprocessing

The job title and description are lowercased, stripped of non-letter characters, and lemmatized after removing common English stopwords.

2. TF-IDF Vectorization

The cleaned text is converted into a 3,000-feature TF-IDF vector using unigrams and bigrams, so short scam phrases (e.g. "processing fee") are captured, not just single words.

3. Machine Learning Model

A Multi-Layer Perceptron (neural network) classifier, trained on ~18,000 real job postings (about 5% labeled fraudulent), outputs a probability that the posting is fake. To counter the class imbalance, the fraudulent examples are oversampled during training only — the reported accuracy is measured on an untouched, realistically-imbalanced test set.

4. Rule-Based Signal

A separate check scans for known scam phrasing, split into high-severity signals (e.g. "registration fee", "wire transfer", "whatsapp only") and lower-severity signals that are common in legitimate postings too (e.g. "work from home", "urgent hiring"). Each is weighted accordingly instead of being treated as an all-or-nothing override.

5. Vagueness Signal

Some scam postings are too thin to catch by wording alone — no named employer, no real way to apply, barely any detail. Three checks run automatically on the text: is it unusually short, does it give any way to actually apply (an email, link, phone number, or phrase like "apply through"), and does it name an identifiable company (a suffix like "Inc"/"LLC"/"Corp", or a "Company:" label). Each missing signal adds the same weight as a low-severity scam phrase — enough that a posting missing all three can tip to Fake, but a single missing signal alone (many short, legitimate gig postings exist) still can't decide anything by itself.

6. AI Reasoning (optional)

When configured, an open-weight LLM (Llama 3.3, via Groq's free API) independently reviews the posting and gives its own fraud-likelihood score plus a plain-English explanation, shown under the result. This is optional infrastructure — if it's unavailable or not configured, detection falls back to the ML + rules + vagueness combination with no change in behavior.

7. Final Decision

All available signals (ML, rules, vagueness, and the AI reasoning score when present) are combined as a probabilistic OR, not a weighted average: a confident judgment from any single source can push a posting to Fake on its own. A single weak or generic phrase still can't decide anything by itself — it only produces a small rule signal that needs real agreement from another source to cross the 50% threshold. This fixes a gap a simple weighted blend had: capping any one signal's influence low enough to stop it from dominating alone also meant that maxed-out evidence from that signal could never cross the threshold on its own whenever the others happened to disagree — even for blatant scam text.

Limits worth knowing

Detection runs entirely on the pasted text — no manual questions, no extra input. That also means signals that aren't in the text can't be used: the single strongest fraud predictor in the raw training data, for instance, is whether the original listing shows a company logo (82% of real postings vs. 33% of fake ones), which is a visual element of the original page and never present in pasted text. A posting with completely clean wording, no scam phrases, and no salary bait can still legitimately score as Real even if it's short or vague — vagueness alone isn't fraud, and a detector tuned to flag every terse posting would just train people to ignore its warnings.
