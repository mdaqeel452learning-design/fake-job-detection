How It Works

1. Preprocessing

The job title and description are lowercased, stripped of non-letter characters, and lemmatized after removing common English stopwords.

2. TF-IDF Vectorization

The cleaned text is converted into a 3,000-feature TF-IDF vector using unigrams and bigrams, so short scam phrases (e.g. "processing fee") are captured, not just single words.

3. Machine Learning Model

A Multi-Layer Perceptron (neural network) classifier, trained on ~18,000 real job postings (about 5% labeled fraudulent), outputs a probability that the posting is fake. To counter the class imbalance, the fraudulent examples are oversampled during training only — the reported accuracy is measured on an untouched, realistically-imbalanced test set.

4. Rule-Based Signal

A separate check scans for known scam phrasing, split into high-severity signals (e.g. "registration fee", "wire transfer", "whatsapp only") and lower-severity signals that are common in legitimate postings too (e.g. "work from home", "urgent hiring"). Each is weighted accordingly instead of being treated as an all-or-nothing override.

5. Final Decision

The ML probability and the rule-based score are combined as a probabilistic OR, not a weighted average: either a confident ML judgment on its own, or overwhelming rule evidence (several severe scam phrases together) on its own, can push a posting to Fake. A single weak or generic phrase still can't decide anything by itself — it only produces a small rule signal that needs real ML agreement to cross the 50% threshold. This fixes a gap a simple weighted blend had: capping the rule engine's influence low enough to stop one generic phrase from dominating also meant that maxed-out rule evidence (multiple severe matches) could never cross the threshold on its own whenever the ML model happened to disagree — even for blatant scam text.

Limits worth knowing

Detection runs entirely on the pasted text — no manual questions, no extra input. That also means signals that aren't in the text can't be used: the single strongest fraud predictor in the raw training data, for instance, is whether the original listing shows a company logo (82% of real postings vs. 33% of fake ones), which is a visual element of the original page and never present in pasted text. A posting with completely clean wording, no scam phrases, and no salary bait can still legitimately score as Real even if it's short or vague — vagueness alone isn't fraud, and a detector tuned to flag every terse posting would just train people to ignore its warnings.
